# Sound Money, Unsound Inferences

### An Austrian reads the September–October 2026 *Misesian*, with Popper and Kahneman along

I read *The Misesian* the way a supporter reads their own team's match report: hoping for a win, but wanting an honest account of the game. I come at economics through Mises and Hayek. I take human action, economic calculation, the knowledge problem and the dangers of fiat money seriously. But I have also learned from two other places. Karl Popper taught me that a claim which nothing could refute explains nothing. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky taught me that the mind making that claim is biased in ways it cannot see.

The September–October 2026 issue (Vol. 3, No. 5) is about education: why government schools fail and why they "don't want you to understand economics." It also has David Gordon's review of Harvey Mansfield's *Where Harvard Went Wrong*, an interview with Jp Cortez of the Sound Money Defense League, and event reports from Albuquerque and Greenville.

My verdict: the issue is right about the big things and careless about the medium-sized ones. And the carelessness is not a matter of style. It is exactly the kind of error Mises and Hayek spent their careers warning about.

---

## 1. Conspiracy vs. spontaneous order: the issue argues with itself

Ryan McMaken's editorial ends on a strong line: *"The regime doesn't want us to understand economics. It's our job to teach what the regime doesn't want us to know."* Paul Cwik's lead essay takes the same line further: *"The takeover of education was not to make us more knowledgeable; it was to make Americans more collectivist."*

A few pages later, Tate Fegley says the opposite, and says it better: *"we do not have to assume that there is any kind of intentionality by those in charge of government schools to keep their students ignorant of good economics. Rather, this is going to be the natural result of a government school."*

Those are two different theories of the same facts. Only one of them is Austrian.

Hayek's central lesson, which he took from Adam Ferguson, is that the most important social patterns are "the result of human action but not of human design." Popper, Hayek's friend and ally, attacked what he called the *conspiracy theory of society*: the habit of explaining every bad social outcome as something a powerful group wanted and arranged. Popper thought the main job of social science was to explain *unintended* consequences. That is exactly what Fegley does. He shows that a tax-funded monopoly, with no profit-and-loss test and with teacher-union incentives, will tend to under-teach the economics that criticizes it. Nobody has to plan it.

The conspiracy version is also weaker evidence. Fegley's incentive argument makes testable predictions: monopoly schools should teach less market economics than schools facing real competition, and union density should track certain curriculum choices. A claim about what "the regime" secretly wants predicts nothing, because any outcome can be read as proof of the hidden intent. When the two explanations compete, the one Popper would accept is the one Mises's own method produces.

**My suggestion to the editors:** lead with Fegley's frame. It is more persuasive, harder to wave away, and true to the tradition the magazine is named after.

## 2. "Parasite class": whose class theory is this?

The editorial says that economics "reveals to us the countless ways that the productive class is continually impoverished by the parasite class."

I know where this comes from. Rothbard and later writers revived a libertarian class theory from Comte and Dunoyer: people who live on taxes versus people who pay them. It has some force. But Mises's own social philosophy rests on the *harmony of rightly understood interests*. In *Human Action* and *Socialism* he argued at length against the idea that society is basically a war between classes. That idea was Marx's, and Mises's answer to it is one of his best contributions.

There is a behavioral cost too. Us-versus-them framing triggers what Kahneman calls System 1, the fast, emotional mode of thinking. It feels like an explanation without the reader having to work through the causal chain. A magazine whose stated purpose is to teach economic reasoning should make readers slow down, not speed up. The better and more Misesian message is that interventionism hurts almost everyone, including many of the people who support it. That message grows the movement. Talk of a "parasite class" mostly rallies people who already agree.

## 3. Cwik's causal chain and the conjunction fallacy

Cwik makes this argument in a few sentences:

> welfare state → family breakdown → (plus) inflation from perpetual deficits forces mothers into work → children raised by non-family → schools fill the void; and deficits are sustained by fiat money, so *"a return to sound money is a cure for many of our social evils."*

Each link is plausible on its own. That is the trap. In their famous "Linda problem" (1983), Tversky and Kahneman showed that people rate a detailed, coherent story as *more* likely than any single part of it. In fact every added link can only lower the probability that the whole chain holds. Six links that are each 70% likely give a chain that is about 12% likely. The story sounds stronger as it gets longer, while the logic gets weaker.

Popper would ask the follow-up question: what would refute this? Cwik's own data suggest an answer. He reports that single-parent households rose to 32% by 1997 and have "fluctuated between 28% and 31% since." But after 1997 the US kept its fiat dollar, ran larger deficits than ever, and (after the 1996 welfare reform) restructured welfare. If fiat money and welfare drive family breakdown, why did breakdown level off just as deficits grew? The essay does not ask. An Austrian should, because Mises insisted that history never "proves" theory but always tests whether we have applied it correctly.

The claim that inflation "has made it necessary for mothers to join the workforce" has the same problem. Women's labor-force participation rose across countries with very different monetary regimes. It was driven by education, contraception, the move to a service economy, rising real wages that raised the opportunity cost of staying home, and, most Austrian of all, *changing subjective preferences*. Inflation may be part of the story. Treating it as the whole story turns subjectivism into determinism.

## 4. The teacher-pay argument: the issue's two essays contradict each other

This is the most important correction, because the economics is the issue's own subject.

Cwik asks teachers: *"what is the educational equivalent of the new machine that increases workers' value marginal product of labor?"* With no such machine, he suggests, "teachers might need to take a pay cut rather than receive a raise."

That is not how wages work in the Austrian theory of factor pricing, and Fegley, eight pages later, says why: *"the minimum they must be paid has to be greater than their opportunity cost in the private sector—otherwise they'd be employed elsewhere."*

Exactly. A barber in 2026 cuts hair no faster than a barber in 1956, yet earns far more in real terms. The reason is not a new machine in the barbershop. It is the many new machines everywhere else, which raised what the barber could earn doing something else. Economists call this Baumol's cost disease. The Austrian version is simpler: a factor's price is set by its *alternative uses* across the whole economy, not only by productivity in its current job. A teacher's real wage can rightly rise with zero gains in classroom productivity. Asking "what's your machine?" applies a firm-level idea to an economy-wide price.

There is an irony as well. Cwik correctly brings in Hayek's knowledge problem ("There is no market price for government schoolteachers"), and then confidently suggests a pay cut. Those can't both stand. If no market price exists, we cannot know teachers are overpaid either. Fegley sees this: *"So I have to remain agnostic on whether teachers are overpaid or underpaid."* That sentence is the most Hayekian line in the issue. It is what intellectual humility in "The Pretence of Knowledge" looks like in practice.

There is also an attribution problem. Cwik's "second secret" is that "the most important factor in student success is the family, not the schools, not the teachers." His "third secret" then implies teachers should be paid according to "recent student outcomes." If families drive most of the outcome, judging teachers by outcomes credits them for what they didn't cause and blames them for what they couldn't prevent. You can hold either secret. Holding both requires ignoring one while arguing the other.

## 5. Evidence by viral video: availability and "what you see is all there is"

Both lead essays cite the same viral clip of a Houston teacher in tears because his seniors could not finish a simple reading task. (One essay says it was on Instagram, the other TikTok.) It is a heartbreaking clip, and Houston's problems are real; Wheatley High was taken over by the state.

But Kahneman would recognize the pattern immediately. The *availability heuristic* leads us to judge how common something is by how easily an example comes to mind, and viral clips are designed to come to mind. *WYSIATI* ("what you see is all there is") is the mind's habit of building a confident story from whatever evidence is in front of it, without asking what is missing. Fegley, to his credit, says his headlines "may be a biased sample." Cwik doesn't.

The same goes for the historical comparison: "In the 1880s and 1890s, literacy rates were between 85% and 90%." Those figures come from census respondents answering *whether they could read and write*. That is a self-reported, minimal standard, nothing like today's grade-level proficiency tests. The overall number also hid huge gaps; illiteracy among Black Americans in 1880 was around 70%. Comparing a self-reported yes/no answer with a demanding modern test, and reading the difference as decline, is a classic measurement error. And Cwik's comparison of his 2026 students with his 1993 students is exactly what *rosy retrospection* predicts: every generation of teachers remembers its earlier students as better.

None of this means government schools are doing fine. It means the case against them should rest on the strong argument (incentives, monopoly, no calculation), not on the weak ones (anecdote, nostalgia, apples-to-oranges statistics).

## 6. Homeschooling: a good case made with bad evidence

I like the homeschooling section. The picture of co-ops, Latin and archery classes, a 35,000-person conference in Winston-Salem, and parents who "freely join these groups and leave as their needs change" is a living example of spontaneous order. Hayek would recognize it, and so does Fegley in his closing point that people need to *see* alternatives exist.

But "Homeschooling... produces the best results" is backed by three children and a statewide estimate that assumes two students per homeschool, taken from an advocacy group. Families who homeschool are a self-selected group: more parental time, often two-parent households, often more education. Their children's results cannot be credited to homeschooling as such. That is basic selection bias, and Cwik's own "second secret" (family matters most) is the very confounder at work.

The Austrian argument for educational freedom doesn't need homeschooling to "win" on test scores. It needs only the claim that parents have the local knowledge and the incentives to find what works for *their* child, and that a competitive process will discover better methods than a monopoly can. That argument is far harder to refute, and it doesn't stake the case on a statistical claim that serious research would complicate.

## 7. Fegley on externalities: the right conclusion, an argument that proves too much

Fegley's essay is the best in the issue. His points on certification as a barrier to entry, on why public-sector unions have outlasted private ones, and on "market failure" arguments that are really disagreements about preferences are sharp and well grounded.

One argument needs fixing. Against Friedman's "neighborhood effects" case for subsidizing schools, Fegley says marginal social benefits "can't" be measured, are "not demonstrated by action," and are "mostly imagined."

Popper's warning applies here: *unmeasurable* is not the same as *nonexistent*. Austrians, of all economists, should be careful with this argument. Subjective value isn't cardinally measurable either, and neither is the deadweight loss of most interventions Austrians oppose. If "you can't measure it" disqualifies externality claims, it disqualifies plenty of Austrian welfare claims too.

The stronger version is Hayek's, not a measurement objection. Even if positive externalities from education exist, no planner can know their size, where they fall, or which subsidy would capture them better than voluntary arrangements would. And public-choice theory explains why any subsidy that is adopted will be shaped by organized producer interests (the unions and teachers' colleges Fegley already describes) rather than by the externality itself. This is a knowledge-and-incentives argument, not a denial that the externalities exist. It wins the debate without handing opponents an easy reply.

Fegley's last point deserves more credit than he gives it. He notes that people tolerate bad schooling because "it's better than nothing," and that seeing real alternatives changes minds more than theory does. That is prospect theory in plain words. People judge options against a reference point (the status quo), and losses loom larger than equal gains. If the imagined alternative to public schools is "nothing," any reform looks like a loss. The fix is to move the reference point by making alternatives visible. That is a serious strategic insight, and it bridges Austrian and behavioral economics. I wish the issue had built on it.

## 8. The Gordon review: honest history needs to be honest about apartheid

The Mises Institute's mission statement, printed in the editorial, includes "honest history." David Gordon's review of Mansfield has good moments, especially on Mansfield's wartime defense of the PATRIOT Act, which Gordon rightly calls Orwellian. But two passages, in my view, work against that mission.

First, Gordon writes that "the campaign against South Africa was the product of Communist propaganda," and cites Mandela's ties to the South African Communist Party. Who supported a cause tells us nothing about whether the cause was right. That is the genetic fallacy. And apartheid was a monstrous violation of exactly what Austrians defend: property rights (the Group Areas Act), freedom of contract and of movement (pass laws), and free labor markets (job reservation). Walter Williams, an economist from our own broad tradition, wrote a whole book, *South Africa's War Against Capitalism*, showing that apartheid was central planning on racial lines. An Austrian should condemn apartheid *on Austrian grounds*, and say so before saying anything about Mandela's affiliations.

Second, the review calls Martin Luther King Jr. a "left-wing icon with Communist connections" and adds a long aside on the Civil War. Whatever one thinks of those debates, they are guilt by association and a distraction from a review about Harvard. They also hand critics an easy excuse to dismiss the economics. That is a high cost for a few lines that add nothing to the argument.

## 9. Sound money: framed as a hypothesis, but still unfalsifiable on Fort Knox

The Cortez interview has a line I want to praise: *"what follows is the hypothesis that if given the choice, people would prefer a money that holds its value."* Calling it a *hypothesis* is exactly right. His bottom-up approach (removing sales and capital-gains taxes on metals and letting people choose) is consistent with Hayek's *Denationalisation of Money*, which argued for competition in currency rather than for a gold standard imposed from above.

So let's test it the way Popper would. We already have a natural experiment. Utah made gold and silver coins legal tender in 2011, and several states have since removed taxes on precious metals. Use of metals for everyday payments has stayed very small. Austrians should be the first to ask why, and Kahneman and Tversky suggest answers: **status quo bias**, **network effects** (money is useful because others accept it), **money illusion** (people think in nominal amounts and barely notice moderate inflation), and the plain fact that taxes are still due in dollars. None of these refutes the case for sound money, but they predict that removing legal barriers will not be enough. A movement that treats its idea as a hypothesis should publish what it expects to see and then check whether it happens.

The Fort Knox section works less well. Rand Paul's tour is dismissed as "not acceptable or sufficient as an audit," which is fair; a tour is not an assay. But the interview never says what *would* settle the matter. If an independent, physical, bar-by-bar assay with a full encumbrance review were done and came back clean, would the Sound Money Defense League accept it? If yes, say so plainly; that turns a suspicion into a testable demand and builds credibility. If nothing could satisfy the doubt, then by Popper's standard it is no longer a factual claim at all.

## 10. The deeper issue: praxeology and history

Underneath all this is a method question Austrians have never fully settled. Mises held that economic theory is *a priori*: true by deduction from the axiom of action, not tested by data. I accept that for the core propositions. Exchange is expected by both parties to benefit them, monopoly privilege blunts the discovery process, and a bureaucracy cannot do profit-and-loss calculation. Fegley's essay rests almost entirely on claims of this kind, which is why it holds up.

But Mises was just as firm, in *Theory and History*, that historical and empirical claims are a different kind of thing. They must be handled with care, statistics, and the understanding of specific actors' motives. Claims like "the government took over education to make us collectivist," "welfare destroyed the family," "inflation pushed mothers into work," "literacy was higher in 1890," and "homeschooling produces the best results" are not praxeological theorems. They are historical and empirical claims, and the authority of the *a priori* core doesn't carry over to them. Much of this issue lets that authority spill over onto claims that haven't earned it.

Hayek saw this. After his long friendship with Popper, he argued in "The Theory of Complex Phenomena" and "The Pretence of Knowledge" that in complex social systems we can make *pattern predictions* but not exact ones, and that intellectual humility is a scientific virtue, not a weakness. The best of the Austrian tradition combines confident theory with modest claims about history. This issue reverses that too often: confident history, and theory used as decoration.

## What a stronger issue would look like

To be constructive, here is what I would want from a future *Misesian* on education:

1. **Lead with incentives, not intentions.** Fegley's "natural result of a government school" is the stronger Austrian argument. Make it the headline.
2. **Separate theorem from history.** Mark clearly which claims come from praxeology and which are empirical, and support the empirical ones with evidence that could have come out the other way.
3. **Engage the strongest counterevidence.** For example, the large-scale studies of the Louisiana voucher program that found *negative* early effects on math scores. An Austrian explanation exists (heavy regulation scared off better private schools, so the "market" was not a free one), but it is only convincing if the magazine raises the evidence itself.
4. **Use behavioral economics as an ally, not a threat.** Status quo bias explains why people stay in failing systems. Reference dependence explains why visible alternatives persuade. Overconfidence explains why central planners overreach. Kahneman's findings are one of the best supports for the Hayekian case against planning, since the planners are subject to the same biases. Austrians should use them.
5. **State what would refute you.** On Fort Knox, on state gold-reserve laws, on homeschool outcomes. A movement confident in its ideas should welcome tests.
6. **Keep "honest history" honest.** Condemn apartheid as an attack on property and markets. Leave guilt by association out of book reviews.

Mises called economics "the hard core of a liberal education." I agree, which is why I hold this magazine to a high standard. The Austrian case for educational freedom and sound money is strong. It is strongest when it trusts its own method and doesn't reach for shortcuts like conspiracy, anecdote, and long causal chains that sound convincing. That is what this issue, in the end, warns its readers about the state.
