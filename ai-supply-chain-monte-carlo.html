Monte Carlo · AI build-out 2026–2030

AI Supply Chain Stress Test

Eleven links run from minerals to monetization, and the AI build-out needs all of them to hold. Each run draws random disruptions for every link, including correlated shocks like a Taiwan Strait crisis. It then asks whether the compute got built and whether AI revenue grew enough to pay for it.

Framework: the “what has to go right” argument in Generative Value’s What has to go right to justify the AI build-out. Every number below is an editable assumption, not a figure from the article. Change any input and the simulation re-runs.

Outcomes

A run is justified when final-year AI revenue covers the build-out’s annual cost (depreciation, cost of capital, operating cost), scaled up by the share of revenue that can pay for infrastructure. It is supply-constrained when the chain delivers less than the threshold share of planned capacity on average.

The chain

Listed upstream to downstream. For each link, set the yearly chance of a disruption, how much capacity it removes (min, most likely, max), and how long it lasts. Headroom is spare capacity against plan: 1.05 absorbs a 5% hit, and 0.95 means the link already falls 5% short. Force lets you pick any one, several or all links: Always adds a guaranteed disruption in the forced-shock year, and Never shields the link completely.

How the model works

  1. Disruptions. Each year, each link breaks with its yearly probability. Severity is drawn from a triangular distribution and duration from a triangle running from half to twice the typical length. A disruption starts at a random month, can spill into the next year, and removes severity × months overlapping ÷ 12 of that year’s capacity. Overlapping disruptions combine multiplicatively.
  2. Supply. Each physical link delivers min(1, headroom − loss) of plan, and the build-out gets the tightest one. Financing caps it too. Deployed capex is plan × min(physical, financing) × discipline. Discipline cuts spending when coverage runs behind the no-shock path.
  3. Demand. AI revenue grows each year at a random rate. A model-progress disruption removes part of that year’s growth, a monetization disruption cuts the revenue level, and both effects persist. Revenue can’t exceed what the installed compute can serve.
  4. Cost. Every capex vintage, including 2023–25, depreciates straight-line over its useful life. Required revenue is (depreciation + WACC × net book + opex × gross book) ÷ infrastructure share of revenue.
  5. Outcome. Final-year coverage against the threshold gives justified or not. Average supply against the threshold gives constrained or not. A systemic break is any year in which supply falls below 50% of plan.

Supply shocks cut both sides of the ledger. Less capex is built, so less revenue is needed to justify it. Scarcity can therefore leave a run justified but supply-constrained. Demand shocks have no such offset.
